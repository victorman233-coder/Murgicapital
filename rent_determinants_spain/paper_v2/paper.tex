\begin{abstract}\noindent
Spanish tenants who stay in their homes have seen rents grow more slowly than local incomes: between 2015 and 2023, the official rent index for all leases in force rose by \insR{} log points and median income per consumption unit by \insY, and the rent-to-income ratio fell in every one of the 703 municipalities covered. Households signing a new lease faced different prices. In 2024, new leases cost \gapVr{} log points more than leases in force per euro of cadastral value, in \gapVrShare\,\% of the larger municipalities; in Catalonia and Aragón, rents at signature outpaced incomes by \catGap{} and \araGap{} log points since 2015. A stock-flow model with long leases, indexed updates and rent resets explains this insider--outsider divergence and predicts that local demand shocks load on entry rents, that incumbents stay while would-be entrants are priced out, and that supply constraints amplify the reset. Using a shift-share instrument for immigration-driven population growth, I find that a cumulative inflow of 1\,\% of the population raises the quality-adjusted entry gap by \bGap\,\% and implied entry rents by \bEntry\,\%, against \bStock\,\% for all leases; income per consumption unit does not respond, household size rises and fewer Spanish nationals move into the municipality, while none move out. The entry gap responds more where land is constrained by sea or slope. Tourism moves dwellings across the same entry margin above a threshold of about \tauHat\,\% of the stock, and rent caps on new leases shift contracts towards unregulated seasonal lets. Spain's rental affordability problem is a problem of access.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Rents top the list of economic concerns in Spain, yet the official index of rents paid by tenants has grown no faster than their incomes. Between 2015 and 2023 the INE rent index, which covers all leases declared in income-tax returns, rose by \insR{} log points; median household income per consumption unit rose by \insY. In none of the 703 municipalities with an official index did rents outpace incomes. The rent faced by a household signing a lease tells a different story. In 2024, new leases cost \gapVr{} log points more than leases in force for dwellings of the same cadastral value, and the gap is positive in \gapVrShare\,\% of the \aeatN{} municipalities with more than 20,000 inhabitants. In Catalonia, where every new lease is registered, rents on new contracts rose by \catR{} log points between 2015 and 2023, \catGap{} more than incomes; in Zaragoza, rents at signature rose by \zgzR{} log points for dwellings on the same street, against \zgzS{} for all leases and \zgzY{} for incomes.

This paper argues that the Spanish rental affordability problem is a problem of entry, explains why it arises and identifies the forces that make it larger in some markets than in others. The starting point is that the average rent of all leases, the object of official statistics, is a poor measure of the cost of finding housing today. Leases last at least five years, existing rents are updated by the consumer price index or by a legal cap, and only a small and falling share of leases is renewed each year: about one in six. In such a market the adjustment to demand shocks falls on the rent reset at the start of a new contract. Incumbent tenants are insulated; households that move, form or arrive pay the market price. The affordability of insiders and the affordability of outsiders diverge.

Section~\ref{sec:theory} formalises this idea in a stock-flow model of a local rental market with contract duration, indexed updates and rent resets, in which the mobility, incomes and size of households respond to the same shocks. The model delivers five predictions. A demand shock raises entry rents more than the average rent of all leases, which absorbs it only at the rate at which leases turn over. Whether the shock worsens affordability depends on whether it raises the income of those who enter, which a measure of income per household can conceal when households grow larger. The response of entry rents is larger where new supply and the reallocation of dwellings from other uses are inelastic. Because incumbents gain from staying as the gap widens, turnover falls and quantities adjust through the households who do not get in rather than through those who leave. Finally, rules that protect incumbents widen the gap between entry rents and the rents of sitting tenants, while caps on entry rents narrow it at the cost of fewer new leases.

The empirical analysis tests these predictions with open administrative data. Tax statistics give, for 2024, the rent, floor area and cadastral value of new leases and of all leases in every municipality above 20,000 inhabitants and in the postal codes of the largest cities, which allows a quality-adjusted measure of the entry gap. Deposit registers in Catalonia, Aragón, the Basque Country and the Comunitat Valenciana record rents at signature; the Aragón register, with \araDep{} residential deposits since 1996 and the street of each dwelling, also reveals how long leases last. The household income atlas gives income per consumption unit and household size by municipality, the household budget survey gives the rent burden of renters, and the statistics of residential changes give moves between municipalities. The demand shock is the immigration-driven inflow of residents predicted by a shift-share instrument based on the settlement of 33 origin groups in 2003 and their national growth \citep{saiz2007,goldsmith2020,borusyak2022}, taken from a companion paper \citep{fernandez2026}.

Six results stand out. First, a cumulative inflow of 1\,\% of the population raises the quality-adjusted entry gap by \bGap\,\% (Anderson-Rubin set \bGapAR) and implied entry rents by \bEntry\,\% \bEntryAR, while the effect on the rent of all leases is \bStock{} and not significant. Second, the same shock does not raise income per consumption unit (\bUc), but it raises household size (\bHs) and therefore income per household (\bHh): measured per household, affordability would appear to improve because more adults share each dwelling. Third, the shock does not push incumbents out: the out-migration of Spanish nationals to other municipalities does not change (\flOutSp), while their in-migration falls by \flInSp{} moves per 100 residents per point of inflow \flInSpAR. Adjustment in quantities, like adjustment in prices, falls on entrants. Fourth, the dwelling stock does not respond, and the entry gap responds more to demand where land is constrained by sea or steep slopes (\rigB{} per standard deviation, $p$ = \rigP) and in coastal municipalities. Fifth, tourism moves dwellings across the same entry margin above a threshold of about \tauHat\,\% of the stock in tourist use: when tourism collapsed in 2020--21, more long-term leases were signed above that threshold, and rents on new leases rose with the recovery. Sixth, regulation acts at the margin the model predicts: the caps on updates of 2022--2024 account for up to \capShare\,\% of the widening of the national entry gap, and the Catalan cap on new-contract rents lowered the number of regulated leases and raised the share of unregulated seasonal leases by \seasPostOne{} percentage points.

The paper contributes to three literatures. It adds to the economics of rental affordability \citep{quigley2004,khametshin2024} a distinction between insiders and outsiders, a model of why they diverge in markets with long indexed leases, and evidence that they do. It contributes to the measurement of rents by showing why indices of all leases \citep{genesove2003,ambrose2015} understate what entrants pay, by how much and for how long, and by constructing a quality-adjusted entry gap from tax data. And it links the literatures on local demand shocks and supply constraints \citep{saiz2010,glaeser2018,hilber2016} and on rent regulation \citep{autor2014,diamond2019,jofre2023} through the margin at which adjustment takes place: in markets that protect incumbents, both prices and quantities adjust at entry.

\section{Institutions and measurement}\label{sec:institutions}

\textit{Leases and updates.} Since March 2019 residential leases in Spain last at least five years when the landlord is a natural person and seven when it is a company, and annual updates of existing leases are usually tied to the consumer price index. Updates were capped at 2\,\% from the end of March 2022 to the end of 2023 and at 3\,\% in 2024, and since 2025 they follow a reference index published by INE. Rents are set freely at the start of a new lease except in markets declared tensioned under the 2023 Housing Act, where they are capped by the rent of the previous lease and a reference index; Catalonia applied this cap in 140 municipalities from 16 March 2024 and in 131 more from October 2024. Seasonal leases, for stays of less than a year for work, study or other temporary reasons, are exempt.

\textit{Measuring rents.} The INE index (IPVA) averages the rents of all leases in force declared in income-tax returns, excluding the Basque Country and Navarre. Since 2021 INE also publishes separate indices for new and existing leases; the implied weight of new leases was \wNewOne\,\% in 2021 and \wNewFour\,\% in 2024. The tax agency publishes, for 2024, the average rent, floor area and cadastral reference value of all leases and of leases signed in the year, by municipality above 20,000 inhabitants and by postal code in the largest cities. Deposit registers record leases at signature in Catalonia (mean rent, municipality and quarter, since 2005), Aragón (one record per deposit with rent, street and whether the deposit has been returned, since 1996), the Basque Country (rent per m$^2$ built, municipality and year, since 2016) and the Comunitat Valenciana (deposit amount, since 2020).

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:lit}

Rental affordability is usually measured as the ratio of rent to the income of renters \citep{quigley2004}. For Spain, \citet{khametshin2024} document the rise in rental demand since 2015 among young and foreign-born households and in large urban and tourist areas, and the high rent burden of low-income tenants, and describe the quantification of its causes as an open question. Indices of rents on all leases move slowly because existing rents are sticky \citep{genesove2003} and because tenants who stay pay less than new ones \citep{ambrose2015}. Rent control turns that wedge into a policy instrument: it benefits incumbent tenants, lowers their mobility and reduces the supply of rental housing to newcomers \citep{autor2014,diamond2019,kholodilin2024}, and in Catalonia it lowered rents and the number of leases signed \citep{jofre2023,perez2026}.

Local demand shocks are capitalised into rents where supply is inelastic \citep{roback1982,saiz2010,glaeser2018,hilber2016,hsieh2019}. Immigration is the standard source of such shocks: \citet{saiz2007} finds that an inflow of 1\,\% of a city's population raises rents by about 1\,\%, and \citet{gonzalez2013} and \citet{sanchis2023} study house prices in Spain. Shift-share designs identify such effects either from the exogeneity of the shocks \citep{borusyak2022,adao2019,borusyak2023} or from that of the initial shares \citep{goldsmith2020}, and immigration instruments based on past settlement can confound current and past inflows \citep{jaeger2018}. Short-term rentals reallocate dwellings from residents to visitors \citep{barron2021}; in Barcelona they raised rents \citep{garcialopez2020}, and in Lisbon the collapse of tourism in 2020 lowered rents and increased the long-term rental supply \citep{batalha2022}. None of these strands separates the rents paid by insiders from those faced by entrants, which is the focus of this paper.

\section{A model of insiders, entrants and rent resets}\label{sec:theory}

The model describes a local rental market $i$ in which leases are long, existing rents follow an update rule and a market price applies only when a dwelling is relet. It has three blocks: an accounting block that links the rents of all leases to entry rents through turnover; a market block that determines entry rents, incomes per consumption unit and household size; and a behavioural block in which turnover itself depends on the gap between the two rents. Throughout, lower-case letters are logs.

\subsection{Leases, resets and the index of all leases}

In year $t$ a share $\lambda_{it}$ of leases ends and the dwelling is relet at the market-clearing entry rent $R^*_{it}$; the remaining leases are updated by $u_t=\min(\pi_t,\bar u_t)$, inflation or a legal cap. The log of the average rent of all leases, $\bar r_{it}$, then satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:index}
\bar r_{it}=\lambda_{it}\,r^*_{it}+(1-\lambda_{it})\,(\bar r_{i,t-1}+u_t),
\end{equation}
and the entry gap $G_{it}\equiv r^*_{it}-\bar r_{it}$ evolves as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap}
G_{it}=(1-\lambda_{it})\,\big[G_{i,t-1}+\Delta r^*_{it}-u_t\big].
\end{equation}
The gap widens whenever entry rents grow faster than the update rule, and it is more persistent the lower the turnover $\lambda$. Two implications follow. First, if entry rents grow at a constant rate $g$ and updates at $u$, the gap converges to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:steady}
G^{\ast}=\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}\,(g-u).
\end{equation}
With the turnover measured in section~\ref{sec:facts}, $(1-\lambda)/\lambda$ is between 4 and 7: each point by which entry rents outgrow the update rule sustains a gap of 4 to 7 points. Second, a permanent shock to entry rents passes to the index of all leases at rate $\lambda$: the index absorbs a share $1-(1-\lambda)^k$ of the shock after $k$ years, about one sixth after one year and a half after four. An index of all leases therefore lags the market, and the lag is longer where leases are longer. Longer minimum durations lower $\lambda$; caps on updates lower $u_t$. Both widen the gap for a given path of entry rents. A cap on entry rents limits $\Delta r^*$ directly.

\subsection{Entry rents, incomes and household size}

Entry rents clear the flow market for new leases. Demand comes from households that form, move or arrive; their number grows with the local population $N_{it}$, and each consumes $q_{it}=A\,(Y_{it}/S_{it})^{\alpha}\,R_{it}^{*\,-\eta}$ units of housing per person, where $Y_{it}/S_{it}$ is income per consumption unit, $S_{it}$ an equivalence scale that increases with household size, and $\eta$ the price elasticity of space per person. A high $\eta$ means that higher rents are absorbed by sharing dwellings. Supply comes from dwellings released by sitting tenants, new construction with elasticity $\sigma_i$, and dwellings reallocated from tourist use or non-use with elasticity $\varepsilon_i$. Log-linearising, a population shock $d\ln N_{it}$ raises entry rents by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:entry}
d\,r^*_{it}=\frac{d\ln N_{it}+\alpha\,(d\ln Y_{it}-d\ln S_{it})}{\eta+\varepsilon_i+\sigma_i},
\end{equation}
and the affordability of entrants, $A^E_{it}=r^*_{it}-\ln(Y_{it}/S_{it})$, changes by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aff}
dA^E_{it}=d\,r^*_{it}-\big(d\ln Y_{it}-d\ln S_{it}\big).
\end{equation}
The affordability of insiders, $A^I_{it}=\bar r_{it}-\ln(Y_{it}/S_{it})$, changes only by $\lambda\,d\,r^*_{it}$ on impact. When the shock comes from arrivals with incomes below the local mean, $d\ln Y_{it}-d\ln S_{it}$ is small or negative, $\eta$ shows up as larger households, and income per household, $d\ln Y_{it}$, rises with household size even if income per consumption unit does not. Tourism enters through $\varepsilon_i$. Tourist demand withdraws dwellings from long-term letting and a collapse of tourist demand returns them; but the stock that can be returned is small where tourist lets are rare, so that $\varepsilon_i$ is close to zero below some share of dwellings in tourist use and positive above it.

\subsection{Turnover, lock-in and who adjusts}

Turnover is not a constant. A sitting tenant who moves within the market gives up a rent that is $G$ below the market, so the probability of moving falls with the gap: $\lambda_{it}=\lambda_0-\kappa\,G_{it}$ with $\kappa>0$, as in the lock-in effects of rent control \citep{diamond2019}. Substituting into \eqref{eq:gap}, a shock that widens the gap also lowers turnover, which makes the gap more persistent and reduces the number of dwellings that come back to the market each year. The flow of vacancies available to entrants shrinks exactly when entry demand rises. Because incumbents are insulated from the reset, a demand shock does not push them out; it rations those who would have entered. In a market with many local municipalities, the households priced out at entry move elsewhere or do not move at all, and the local in-migration of other entrants falls.

\subsection{Regulation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:gap} gives the mechanical effect of a cap on updates: it lowers $u_t$ and widens the gap one for one for the leases updated while it binds. A forward-looking landlord adds a second effect. If the landlord sets the entry rent so that the expected real rent over the life of the lease is unchanged, and expects updates to fall short of inflation by $\pi-\bar u$ in each year of the lease, the entry rent rises by approximately $(\pi-\bar u)\,\bar k$, where $\bar k=\beta(1-\lambda)/[1-\beta(1-\lambda)]$ is the discounted expected time elapsed within a lease and $\beta$ the discount factor. With $\lambda$ = 0.18 and $\beta$ = 0.96, $\bar k$ is about 3.7 years. A permanent cap therefore raises entry rents by front-loading; a temporary cap, like the Spanish one, mainly widens the gap mechanically. A cap on entry rents lowers $r^*$ in the regulated segment but lowers the return on letting there, so owners who can move dwellings to unregulated uses, such as seasonal lets, do so, and the number of regulated leases falls.

\subsection{Predictions}

\begin{description}
\item[P1 (entry loading).] A demand shock raises entry rents by more than the rent of all leases, which absorbs it at the rate $\lambda$, so that the entry gap $G$ widens.
\item[P2 (income measurement).] A shock that raises household size $S$ raises income per household mechanically without raising income per consumption unit. Affordability measured per household can improve while the affordability of entrants deteriorates.
\item[P3 (amplification).] The response of entry rents is larger where new supply ($\sigma_i$) and reallocation ($\varepsilon_i$) are less elastic: in land-constrained markets and where few dwellings can be moved from other uses into long-term letting. Reallocation from tourist use is non-linear in the share of tourist lets.
\item[P4 (who adjusts).] Incumbents do not leave in response to a demand shock; quantities adjust through lower in-migration of other would-be entrants, and turnover falls as the gap widens.
\item[P5 (regulation).] Caps on updates widen the gap; caps on entry rents narrow it and reduce the number of regulated leases, shifting lets towards unregulated uses.
\end{description}

\subsection{Incidence}

The model also says who bears the cost of a demand shock. With $\lambda$ the share of households that sign a lease in a year, the extra rent paid in that year is $\lambda\,d\,r^*$ per dwelling, all of it by entrants, while sitting tenants pay only the update. As long as the gap persists, the burden of the shock is shared between entrants and landlords, and sitting tenants gain relative to the market. Affordability statistics based on all leases or on all renters average the two groups with weights $\lambda$ and $1-\lambda$ and therefore show only about a sixth of the change faced by entrants in the year of the shock.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\textit{Entry gap.} The main outcome is the quality-adjusted entry gap in 2024 from the tax statistics of dwellings declared in income-tax returns:
\[
G^{VR}_i=\ln\!\Big(\frac{\text{rent}^{new}_i}{\text{value}^{new}_i}\Big)-\ln\!\Big(\frac{\text{rent}^{all}_i}{\text{value}^{all}_i}\Big),
\]
where value is the mean cadastral reference value of the dwellings. $G^{VR}$ equals the log ratio of gross yields on new and on all leases. A version per m$^2$ and the raw gap are used for robustness. In Spain as a whole, new leases averaged \aeatRentNew{} euros per month against \aeatRent{} for all leases, while new leases were on dwellings of lower value (\aeatVrNew{} against \aeatVr{} euros): the gross yield was \aeatYldNew\,\% against \aeatYld\,\%. The same statistics by postal code cover \cpN{} postal codes in \cpCities{} cities.

\textit{Rents, incomes and moves over time.} INE indices for all, new and existing leases; deposit registers in Catalonia, Aragón, the Basque Country and the Comunitat Valenciana; the INE household income atlas (2015--2023) for median net income per consumption unit, income per person and per household, and average household size; the INE household budget survey (2016--2024) for the rents, incomes and floor area of renting households (\epfN{} household-years); and the INE statistics of migrations and changes of residence for moves between municipalities by nationality (2021--2024). In Aragón, I match \araMatch\,\% of residential deposits to municipalities and build a composition-adjusted index of entry rents from a regression of log rent on year effects and street fixed effects within each municipality.

\textit{Demand shock and controls.} The cumulative net inflow of foreign-born residents between 2015 and 2024 relative to 2015 population, from the municipal register (to 2021) and the annual census (from 2021), using within-source changes. The instrument sums the companion paper's annual shift-share predictions over the same years. Controls are predetermined characteristics from the 2011 census and 2012 firm counts.

\textit{Supply constraints, tourism, regulation and tenure.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Supply constraints: share of land within 10 km of each municipality that is sea or has a slope above 15\,\%, computed from the Copernicus 90-metre digital elevation model in the spirit of \citet{saiz2010}. Cadastral dwelling counts measure the supply response.
\item Tourism: tourist dwellings as a share of all dwellings (INE, August 2020).
\item Regulation: seasonal leases by municipality and quarter in Catalonia (2023--2026); CPI and the legal caps on updates.
\item Tenure: appraised housing values by municipality (Ministry of Housing) relative to income, as a measure of exposure to the rise in mortgage rates.
\end{itemize}
All data are public and are downloaded by the replication code.

\section{Insiders and entrants}\label{sec:facts}

Table~\ref{tab:facts} and figure~\ref{fig:facts} set out the central facts. For all leases, rents grew by \insR{} log points between 2015 and 2023 and income per consumption unit by \insY: the rent-to-income ratio of sitting tenants fell by \insGap{} points on average, by \insGapA{} in municipalities of 10,000--20,000 inhabitants and by \insGapE{} in the largest cities. Not a single municipality shows the opposite. The household budget survey confirms this for renters as a whole: their median rent-to-income ratio was \epfBurdOne\,\% in 2016 and \epfBurdNine\,\% in 2024, and the share paying more than 40\,\% of income fell from \epfOverOne\,\% to \epfOverNine\,\%.

New leases tell a different story in three of the four regions with registers. In Catalonia, rents on new leases rose by \catR{} log points against \catY{} for incomes, and they outpaced incomes in \catShare\,\% of municipalities. The gap between entry rents and incomes increases with the size of the municipality (figure~\ref{fig:facts}b). In Aragón, where the street fixed effects hold the location of the dwelling constant, rents at signature rose by \araR{} log points against \araY{} for incomes and \araS{} for all leases; they outpaced incomes in \araShare\,\% of the \araN{} municipalities with enough leases and outpaced the index of all leases in every one of the \araNs{} that have one (figure~\ref{fig:aragon}a). In the Comunitat Valenciana, deposits on new leases rose by \valR{} points between 2020 and 2023, against \valY{} for incomes. In the Basque Country, however, rents per m$^2$ on new leases rose by \basR{} points between 2016 and 2023, less than incomes (\basY), and they outpaced incomes in only \basShare\,\% of municipalities: the divergence at entry is not universal.

\section{Empirical strategy}\label{sec:strategy}

For municipality $i$ in province $p$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:est}
y_i=\beta\, x_i+\bm{w}_i'\bm\gamma+\delta_p+\varepsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ is the cumulative foreign-born inflow relative to 2015 population and $y_i$ is, in turn, the entry gap, the growth of the rent of all leases, implied entry-rent growth (the sum of the two), income per consumption unit, income per person and per household, household size, population, the dwelling stock and moves between municipalities. Affordability outcomes are rent growth minus growth of income per consumption unit, for insiders (all leases) and for entrants (implied entry rents). $x_i$ is instrumented with the cumulative shift-share prediction $z_i=\sum_t\sum_o \ell_{io}\,(g_{ot}-g_{opt})/P_i$, where $\ell_{io}$ is municipality $i$'s share of the 2003 national stock of origin $o$ and $g_{ot}-g_{opt}$ the national change in that stock excluding province $p$. Regressions are weighted by 2015 population, standard errors are clustered by province and Anderson-Rubin sets are reported throughout. The pre-period falsification regresses rent growth in 2012--2015 on the 2015--2024 inflow.

The instrument can be justified by the exogeneity of the origin shocks \citep{borusyak2022} or by that of the 2003 settlement shares \citep{goldsmith2020}. In a long difference there is a single realisation of 33 origin shocks, and the shocks are heavy-tailed because some origins had few residents in 2003. Section~\ref{sec:robust} shows that shock-based inference \citep{adao2019,borusyak2023} is uninformative in this setting, so the identifying assumption is in practice that the 2003 shares of the origins that carry the instrument (Colombia, Peru and Romania, with \rotTop\,\% of the Rotemberg weight) are unrelated to later changes in the entry gap, conditional on controls. I assess it with the sum-of-shares control recommended by \citet{borusyak2022}, by dropping each of these origins, with balance and pre-period tests, and with an annual panel in which shocks vary by year.

Prediction P3 is tested by interacting the inflow with standardised measures of geographic constraints and instrumenting the interaction with $z_i$ times the constraint. Geographic constraints are predetermined and, unlike density or construction, do not reflect past demand.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Where a demand shock goes}

Table~\ref{tab:main} and figure~\ref{fig:shock} report the effects of the demand shock. A cumulative inflow of 1\,\% of the population raises the quality-adjusted entry gap by \bGap\,\% (Anderson-Rubin set \bGapAR; first-stage $F$ \bGapF), the gap per m$^2$ by \bGapM\,\% and the raw gap by \bGapRaw\,\%. Combined with the response of the rent of all leases, \bStock\,\% \bStockAR, the implied response of entry rents is \bEntry\,\% \bEntryAR: entry rents respond several times more than the average rent of all leases, as prediction P1 implies.

The shock raises population by \bPop\,\% per point of inflow: most of the inflow adds to the population, and the rest is offset by fewer arrivals of other residents (section~\ref{sec:who}). It does not raise income per consumption unit (\bUc, \bUcAR) or income per person (\bPp). It raises household size by \bHs\,\% \bHsAR{} and income per household by \bHh\,\% \bHhAR. Prediction P2 holds: households absorb the shock partly by sharing dwellings, which raises household income without raising the income available per person. The affordability of entrants deteriorates by \bAE\,\% per point of inflow \bAEAR, while that of insiders does not change significantly (\bAI). The dwelling stock does not respond (\bViv, \bVivAR), and the falsification test passes: the inflow of 2015--2024 does not predict rent growth in 2012--2015 (\bPre, \bPreAR).

\subsection{Who adjusts}\label{sec:who}

Prediction P4 concerns quantities. Table~\ref{tab:margins}, panel A, uses moves between municipalities in 2021--2024, instrumented with the shift-share prediction for the same years. A foreign-born inflow of one point does not change the out-migration of Spanish nationals to other municipalities (\flOutSp, \flOutSpAR, against a mean of \flOutSpM{} moves per 100 residents over the four years); it lowers their in-migration from other municipalities by \flInSp{} \flInSpAR, so that their net migration falls by \flNetSp{} \flNetSpAR. Foreign nationals move more in both directions. Incumbents stay; the households who would have moved in from elsewhere do not. Adjustment in quantities, like adjustment in prices, falls on entrants.

\subsection{Supply constraints}

Table~\ref{tab:rigidity} and figure~\ref{fig:rigidity} test prediction P3. A one-standard-deviation increase in the share of undevelopable land within 10 km raises the effect of the demand shock on the entry gap by \rigB{} ($p$ = \rigP) and on implied entry rents by \rigEntry{} ($p$ = \rigEntryP). The interaction is similar for the sea (\rigSea, $p$ = \rigSeaP) and for steep slopes (\rigSteep, $p$ = \rigSteepP), and it survives the inclusion of the interaction between the inflow and tourist exposure (\rigBt, $p$ = \rigPt). The geographic constraint does not change the response of the rent of all leases (\rigStock) or of the cadastral dwelling stock (\rigViv), which is close to zero everywhere. The amplification works through the entry margin, not through measured construction. Two caveats apply: the partial first-stage $F$ of the interaction is modest (\rigFi{} for the composite measure), and the marginal effect on the entry gap is only clearly positive above about 60\,\% of undevelopable land. Table~\ref{tab:margins}, panel B, points the same way: the response of the entry gap is \hetCoast{} larger (standard error \hetCoastSe) in coastal municipalities, and it does not differ by size or between core cities and their peripheries. The response of the rent of all leases, by contrast, is smaller in large cities (\hetBigS, standard error \hetBigSse) and in core cities (\hetCoreS, \hetCoreSse), where leases are longer and turnover lower.

\subsection{Tourism}

The interaction between the inflow and tourist exposure in table~\ref{tab:rigidity} is negative (\tourInt, $p$ = \tourIntP): the same demand shock widens the entry gap less where many dwellings are tourist lets, as if part of that stock acted as a reserve for long-term letting. The collapse of tourism in 2020--21 tests this margin directly (table~\ref{tab:channels}, panel A; figure~\ref{fig:tourism}). In Catalan municipalities where 3--8\,\% and more than 8\,\% of dwellings were tourist lets, the number of new long-term leases rose by \tNbtwocovid\,\% and \tNbthreecovid\,\% during the collapse relative to municipalities with little tourist letting. With the recovery, rents on new leases rose by about 3\,\% in the same groups (\tRbtworecov\,\% and \tRbthreerecov\,\%). Below 3\,\% of dwellings there is no reallocation of leases.

A threshold regression \citep{hansen2000} lets the data choose where the response changes. Allowing the slope of the collapse, recovery and later effects in tourist exposure to differ above and below a threshold, least squares locates it at \tauHat\,\% of dwellings for new leases signed and for new-lease rents (likelihood-ratio set \tauRentLo--\tauRentHi\,\% for rents); a wild cluster bootstrap rejects a common slope ($p$ \tauPn{} for leases signed, \tauPr{} for rents). \tauAbove{} of the \tauMunis{} Catalan municipalities lie above it. Tourism shapes entry rents where tourist lets are a sizable share of the stock, as prediction P3 implies for a reallocation elasticity that is close to zero in markets with few tourist lets.

\subsection{Regulation}

Prediction P5 says that caps on updates widen the entry gap and that caps on entry rents narrow it. Between 2021 and 2024 the national entry gap widened in every year, from \gapTwoOne{} to \gapTwoFour{} log points. In 2022 existing leases rose by \exTwoTwo\,\% while inflation was \piTwoTwo\,\%. Adding back to existing leases the difference between inflation and the cap, for the share of each year in which the cap applied (\capWedge{} points in 2022 and \capWedgeB{} in 2023), would have left a gap of \capGcf{} points in 2024: the caps account for at most \capShare\,\% of the widening, an upper bound because not all leases are indexed to the CPI (table~\ref{tab:margins}, panel C). The rest reflects faster growth of entry rents, which in 2024 rose by 8.8\,\% against 2.8\,\% for existing leases. Provincial data do not show the decoupling the cap might have produced: across provinces, the growth of existing and of new leases was more correlated in 2022 (\corrExTwo) than in 2021 (\corrExOne), so the cap held the level of existing-lease growth down without severing its link to local markets.

The Catalan cap on new leases, evaluated in the companion analysis with stacked difference-in-differences, lowered new-lease rents by about 5\,\% and the number of new leases by about 18\,\% in the first wave. Table~\ref{tab:channels}, panel B, shows where part of those leases went: after the cap, the share of seasonal leases among new leases rose by \seasPostOne{} percentage points in the first wave (seasonal leases are exempt from the cap) and seasonal leases rose by about \seasLnOne\,\%. In the whole sample their share rose from \seasShareTwoThree\,\% in 2023 to \seasShareTwoFive\,\% in 2025. The register of seasonal leases begins in 2023, so the pre-period is short (figure~\ref{fig:cap}).

\subsection{Tenure, spillovers and renters}

If the rise in mortgage rates from 2022 had pushed would-be buyers into renting, rents should have risen more where housing was most expensive relative to incomes. Table~\ref{tab:channels}, panel C, finds no break after 2022 in the index of all leases, and no association between price-to-income and the entry gap in 2024; the tenure channel is not supported by these data. Panel D studies spillovers within functional urban areas: the demand shock of the core city raises the dwelling stock of its peripheral municipalities (\spCoreViv, $p$ = \spCoreVivP) but has small and imprecise effects on their rents and entry gaps. Moves tell the same story: the core's shock raises the in-migration of foreign nationals to the periphery (\spInFxCore{} per point, $p$ = \spInFxCoreP) but not that of Spanish nationals (\spInSpCore, standard error \spInSpCoreSe). Supply in the periphery responds to demand in the core; rents there do not measurably follow.

The household budget survey does not identify recent movers, but households headed by a foreign national are likelier to have signed recently. Conditional on region, municipality size and floor area, their rent per m$^2$ was \epfFprem\,\% relative to other renters in 2016--2018 and rose by \epfFchg{} points (standard error \epfFchgSe, $p$ = \epfFchgP) by 2022--2024, while that of young Spanish-headed households did not change (\epfYchg, \epfYchgSe). The survey is consistent with an entry premium but, with these proxies, the evidence is weak.

\section{Robustness}\label{sec:robust}

\textit{Shift-share identification.} Table~\ref{tab:robust}, panel A, adds the 2003 foreign-born share, scaled as the instrument, which \citet{borusyak2022} recommend when shares do not sum to one: the entry gap responds by \ssGap{} \ssGapAR{} and implied entry rents by \ssEntry{} \ssEntryAR, with a weaker first stage ($F$ = \ssGapF); the rent of all leases responds by \ssStock{} \ssStockAR. Dropping from the instrument any of the five origins with the largest Rotemberg weights leaves the effect on the entry gap between \looMin{} and \looMax, with Anderson-Rubin sets that exclude zero. The 2003 shares of Colombia and Peru are unrelated to rent growth in 2012--2015 conditional on the controls ($t$ = \balColPre{} and \balPerPre), but the Romanian share is associated with slower pre-period growth ($t$ = \balRomPre); without Romania the effect is unchanged.

Shock-based inference is not informative here. Recentring the instrument by permuting the 33 origin shocks across origins removes its first stage ($F$ = \riFall), because the realised shocks are dominated by a few origins with small 2003 stocks; permuting within continents keeps a first stage ($F$ = \riFcont) but yields unbounded randomisation sets, and exposure-robust sets \citep{adao2019} are unbounded too. The long-difference estimates therefore rest on the exogeneity of settlement shares \citep{goldsmith2020}, with the usual caveat that past settlement may predict later inflows through persistent local conditions \citep{jaeger2018}. A placebo by origin points the same way but has little power: inflows from high-income European origins, which more often buy than rent, do not raise the entry gap (\plRich, standard error \plRichSe) while the rest do (\plOth, \plOthSe), but the first stage for the former is weak ($F$ = \plRichF).

\textit{Annual panel.} Panel B uses the companion paper's annual panel, in which origin shocks vary by year and randomisation inference is informative, merged with annual rents at signature in Catalonia, Aragón and the Comunitat Valenciana (\pnN{} municipality-years, \pnMunis{} municipalities). An inflow of 1\,\% raises new-lease rents by \pnNew{} and the rent of all leases by \pnStock, so that the entry gap widens by \pnGap. The estimates are imprecise ($F$ = \pnF; randomisation $p$-values \pnRIpNew{} for entry rents and \pnRIpGap{} for the gap), and next year's predicted inflow is negatively related to this year's entry rents (\pnLead, standard error \pnLeadSe), a sign that annual changes in rents at signature are noisy. The panel corroborates the direction of the cross-sectional results, not their magnitude.

\textit{Instrument, weights and registers.} Panel C reports alternative instruments, weights and registers. With the companion paper's instrument, which sums annual predictions with centred timing over the 699 municipalities of its sample, the effect on the rent of all leases is larger (\ciStock) and the effect on the quality-adjusted entry gap is \ciGap{} \ciGapAR; the raw gap responds by \ciGapRaw{} \ciGapRawAR. Without population weights the first stage is weak (\unwGapF{} for the entry gap), and the estimates apply to the larger municipalities that hold most of the population. In the Basque register, which measures rents per m$^2$, the demand shock raises entry rents by \basIv{} \basIvAR{} and worsens entrants' affordability by \basAE{} \basAEAR. The Catalan register illustrates why quality adjustment matters: its mean rent per contract falls with the demand shock (\catIv, \catIvAR), consistent with arrivals shifting new leases towards cheaper and smaller dwellings, which the quality-adjusted tax measure removes.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textit{What the evidence supports.} Rental affordability in Spain deteriorated at entry and not for sitting tenants, in every source that observes rents at signature except the Basque register. Local demand shocks load on entry rents, more where geography constrains supply, while incomes per consumption unit do not respond and households grow larger. Incumbents do not leave; fewer households move in. Leases turn over slowly enough for the index of all leases to lag the market by years. Tourism and rent caps act on the same entry margin.

\textit{What it does not establish.} The deterioration at entry is not universal: in the Basque Country, entry rents per m$^2$ grew less than incomes. The quality-adjusted entry gap is observed in levels only for 2024, so its response to demand rests on a cross-section and on the assumption that the gap was unrelated to the predicted inflow before the shock; the pre-period test on all leases supports, but does not prove, it. The long-difference instrument is identified from 2003 settlement shares dominated by three origins rather than from as-good-as-random shocks, and the annual panel that uses shock variation is imprecise. Incomes are those of all households in the municipality, not those of entrants; the income of the households who sign new leases is not observed in open data. The tenure channel and spillovers on rents are not supported.

\textit{Welfare.} A larger entry gap transfers resources from movers, young households and new arrivals to landlords and to sitting tenants, and it discourages mobility: households that would gain from moving for a job stay put, and those who need to move pay the reset. Larger households are an adjustment margin that the companion paper documents, not by itself a welfare loss. Caps on updates and longer leases protect insiders and widen the gap; caps on entry rents narrow it but reduce the number of leases offered on the regulated market and shift lets towards seasonal contracts.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The rise in Spanish rents is concentrated on households that enter the rental market. Official statistics show rents of sitting tenants growing more slowly than incomes everywhere; tax data and deposit registers show rents at signature growing faster than incomes in most markets and a quality-adjusted gap of about 16 log points between new leases and leases in force in 2024. The two facts are consistent: in a market of long, indexed leases, of which fewer than one in five turn over each year, the average rent of all leases absorbs only a fraction of what entrants pay, and with a lag of several years.

The paper explains the divergence with a stock-flow model in which local demand shocks are absorbed by rent resets, and tests its predictions with an immigration-driven demand shock. Entry rents respond several times more than the rents of all leases; incomes per consumption unit do not respond and households grow larger; incumbents stay and fewer households move in; the reset is larger where land is scarce and where tourism can withdraw dwellings from long-term letting; and regulation that protects incumbents widens the gap while caps at signature shift lets towards unregulated contracts. Prices and quantities adjust at entry.

The findings have three implications for policy. First, measurement: an index of all leases is the right deflator for what sitting tenants pay, but the wrong indicator of access. Statistics of rents at signature, adjusted for the quality and location of dwellings, exist in four regions and in the tax data for one year; publishing them annually for all of Spain would show where the problem is and how fast it grows. Second, supply: because the shock is absorbed at entry, measures that add dwellings to the flow available to entrants, through construction where land allows it and through the conversion of underused dwellings where it does not, act where the problem arises, while measures aimed at sitting tenants do not reach it. Third, regulation: each instrument moves the gap in a predictable direction. Caps on updates and longer minimum durations protect insiders and widen the gap; caps at signature narrow it in the regulated segment but reduce the number of regulated leases and shift lets to segments the cap does not cover. A policy mix that protects tenants without closing the market to newcomers has to combine regulation with an expansion of the entry flow.

Two extensions would sharpen these conclusions. Linking successive deposits on the same dwelling would measure the reset directly and remove the need for quality adjustment, and observing the incomes of the households who sign new leases would measure the affordability of entrants rather than infer it from local averages. Both require administrative data that the registers already hold.
